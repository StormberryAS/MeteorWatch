# MeteorWatch

Deterministic meteor shower tracker. MeteorWatch counts down to the next major annual meteor shower peak and shows how much of the Moon is lit at that moment, rated as a rough guide to how dark the sky will be, without any external APIs. The page comes from the web; the calculation then runs in your browser and nothing is sent to a server. There is no service worker, so the page does not promise to open again without a connection.

**Live:** [meteor.stormberry.as](https://meteor.stormberry.as)

## Features
- **Embedded Showers**: nine major showers (Quadrantids, April Lyrids, Eta Aquariids, Southern Delta Aquariids, Perseids, Orionids, Leonids, Geminids and Ursids). The names are the full names of the IMO Working List with the Greek letters spelled out.
- **Peak table**: the peak of each shower in each year from 2026 to 2037, as a UTC instant to the minute, in `ts/peaks.ts`. An instant is the moment the Sun's ecliptic longitude equals the longitude the International Meteor Organization (IMO) gives for the shower (Working List of Visual Meteor Showers, Table 5 of its 2026 and 2027 calendars, equinox J2000.0). The table is generated by `tools/gen-peaks.py` (VSOP87, standard-library Python); never edit it by hand. Each row also holds the shower's radiant at the peak (right ascension and declination, mean equinox of the peak date), interpolated from IMO's radiant drift table (Table 6, page 26 of both calendars, equinox J2000.0) and precessed to the peak date by the same generator (Meeus, chapter 21); the city filter uses it. Real maxima can differ by hours from year to year, so treat a peak as the centre of the best window.
- **Countdown**: real-time countdown to the next peak instant, calculated in your browser. The peak is shown as date and time in your own time zone, with the zone's name. After its peak a shower stays on the card for 12 hours, marked "Peak passed" with how long ago the peak was and which shower comes next, before the countdown moves on. The page shows a clear message when the table runs out (after the end of 2037).
- **Moon at the peak**: `suncalc.js` gives the Moon's phase and illuminated fraction as seen from the centre of the Earth (no location is used) at the peak instant. The page rates it EXCELLENT (under 20% lit), GOOD (20 to 59%) or POOR (60% or more), using the rounded percentage it shows. The rating ignores the Moon's altitude, the radiant's altitude, twilight and your latitude.
- **ZHR**: IMO's zenithal hourly rate, the ideal rate under a perfectly dark sky with the radiant overhead. It is not what an observer sees. The "best region" line says in which hemisphere the radiant is better placed.
- **City filter (optional)**: pick a city from the built-in list of 25,007 cities (no location permission; the list comes from this site, and nothing about your city is sent anywhere). The countdown then skips showers that cannot be seen from that city, and times show in its time zone. The choice is stored only in this browser (`localStorage`, never sent anywhere), and a "Clear city" button removes it. Without a city the page behaves as before: all nine showers, times in your own time zone. A shower counts as visible from a city when, within 12 hours before or after its peak (sampled every 5 minutes), the Sun is at least 12 degrees below the horizon and the radiant is at least 15 degrees above it, for 30 minutes or more in total (`ts/visibility.ts`; geometric altitudes, SunCalc for the Sun). The rule is a guide, not a promise:
  - It ignores clouds and weather, the Moon, light pollution, hills, trees and buildings.
  - It ignores the real time of the maximum: IMO gives the Quadrantid peak about 2.5 hours of uncertainty, and several showers have broad maxima. It looks only at the 12 hours either side of the peak instant, not at other nights of the shower's activity.
  - It ignores the height of the city above sea level and the refraction of the air. The radiant is IMO's mean radiant (Table 6) for the peak instant, precessed to the mean equinox of the peak date (no nutation, no aberration; the generator's own check is the worked example of Meeus, chapter 21). The largest remaining error of the calculation is the Sun's altitude from SunCalc, about 0.2 degrees.
  - "Visible" means that the radiant is up in a dark sky. It does not promise a count of meteors. The ZHR note on the card stays, and the Moon rating still describes the Moon as seen from the centre of the Earth.
  - Where it differs from IMO's words: it shows the Eta Aquariids up to about 43 to 45 degrees north (IMO: useful south of about 40 degrees north), the Perseids up to about 63 degrees north (IMO: not north of about 60 degrees north) and the Southern Delta Aquariids up to about 57 to 58 degrees north (for example Göteborg, where the radiant reaches only about 17 degrees in a dark sky; IMO: only a small part of the shower is seen at mid-northern latitudes), and it hides the April Lyrids at Tromsø (the Sun stays above minus 8 degrees there). The research behind the rule compared it with every explicit IMO statement about where a shower can be seen, for ten cities in 2026 and 2027; the page's calculation is held to a Skyfield (JPL DE440s) oracle in `tests/visibility-vectors.json` (the same visible or hidden result in all 180 city and shower cells, minutes within 10; these cells are far from the limits, and the edge cells of `tests/visibility-edge-vectors.json` are checked for the same result).
  - Near a limit the number of minutes is approximate, and so is the result itself. A fixed threshold of 12 and 15 degrees, a Sun altitude that is known to about 0.2 degrees and a window of 12 hours either side of the peak mean that, for a city near a limit, the minutes on the card can differ from a precise calculation by 25 minutes or more, and the same city can see a shower in one year and not in the next (for example Melbourne and the Leonids, or Luleå and the April Lyrids). Do not read the minutes as exact.
- **Responsive Layout**: optimised for mobile and desktop with a cinematic indigo glassmorphism theme.

## Architecture
- **Vanilla HTML and CSS, TypeScript**, no frameworks.
- **TypeScript source in `ts/`, compiled JavaScript committed.** `ts/app.ts` compiles to `app.js`, at the same path the page has always loaded (and the generated `ts/peaks.ts` to `peaks.js`, loaded before it), as a classic script (no modules). The compiled file is committed because GitHub Pages serves the repository as it is, with no build step of its own; `.gitattributes` marks it as generated. Edit the TypeScript, never the `.js`.
- **SunCalc vendored.** `suncalc.js` is SunCalc 1.8.0, kept byte for byte and marked as vendored; `ts/suncalc.d.ts` types it for the TypeScript.
- **City catalogue copied, loaded when used.** `cities.js` is the shared Stormberry Labs city catalogue (25,007 cities; fields name, country, lat, lon, time zone), written by SunApp's `update_cities.py` into every app repository of its list and copied here byte for byte, never edited (`.gitattributes` marks it as generated; `tools/check-city-parity.py` pins its sha256 and shape and, when a sibling `SunApp/cities.js` exists, compares the two files). It is about 1.1 MB (about 400 KB gzip), so `index.html` does not load it: `ts/app.ts` adds it to the page, from the same origin, the first time the person uses the city field (focus, pointer or typing; pressing "Clear city" does not start the load), and `bump_assets.py` stamps the address in the compiled `app.js` with the file's hash (`./cities.js?v=...`, as SunApp does for its service worker). A return visit with a stored city does not load it until the person uses the field. The catalogue is decoded only when a query is typed. The city search is MoonApp's (accent-insensitive both ways, exonym-aware, prefix matches first, at most 8 rows), ported with MoonApp's function names; `tests/search-vectors.json` is MoonApp's frozen set of 162 queries, copied byte for byte, and `tests/search.test.ts` holds this page to it.
- **Visibility rule.** `ts/visibility.ts` compiles to `visibility.js`, loaded between `peaks.js` and `app.js`: Greenwich sidereal time (Meeus, formula 12.4), the radiant's altitude, and the count of dark radiant minutes in the 289 samples around a peak. The page asks for it only for the peaks that the card needs (at most about 11 for one city) and keeps the results until the city changes.
- **Peak table.** `python3 tools/gen-peaks.py` writes `ts/peaks.ts`; `python3 tools/gen-peaks.py --check` fails when the committed file is not what a fresh run writes (`npm run check` and the tests run it). The method is in the generator's header: the Earth's position from the VSOP87B series (the J2000 version, `tools/vsop87b-extract.py` reproduces the embedded terms from the IMCCE file), FK5 correction, geometric longitude (no aberration, which would move every instant about 8 minutes later), Delta T from the Espenak and Meeus polynomial, rounded to the minute. To extend the range, change `FIRST_YEAR` and `LAST_YEAR` in the generator and run it. A test fails after 1 January 2036, so the table is renewed with two years to spare (an APK release stays frozen for as long as it is installed).
- **Build:** `npm run build` (`tools/build-ts.sh`) installs the pinned compiler (`npm ci`), compiles, then runs `bump_assets.py` to restamp the `?v=` cache stamps. `npm run check` changes nothing and fails when the committed JavaScript is not what the TypeScript compiles to. `npm test` runs the tests in `tests/` with the test runner built into Node 24. The published app has no runtime dependencies.
- **Privacy First**, no cookies, no tracking. Zero external API calls. The only thing the page keeps on the device is the city you pick, in `localStorage` under one key (`meteorwatch.city`), and only when you pick one; every access is in a try and catch, so the page works the same without storage.
- Stormberry dark-mode glassmorphism design system, Inter typography.
- **Sovereign AI**, built and maintained using high-speed agentic workflows.

## Stack
- Browser `Date` for real-time countdown calculations.
- The generated table of peak instants in `ts/peaks.ts` (IMO data, VSOP87 for the Sun).
- [SunCalc](https://github.com/mourner/suncalc) for the Moon's phase and illuminated fraction (geocentric) and, with a city, for the Sun's altitude.
- The shared city catalogue in `cities.js` (GeoNames and Kartverket data, CC BY 4.0) for the optional city filter.
- [Inter](https://rsms.me/inter/) typeface, locally hosted.

## Local development
```bash
git clone https://github.com/StormberryAS/MeteorWatch.git
cd MeteorWatch
python3 -m http.server 3004
```
Open `http://localhost:3004` in your browser.
